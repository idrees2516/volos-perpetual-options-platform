Deep product blueprint: building the missing “Volatility OS” for onchain options
After drilling into the current infrastructure, APIs, RFQ systems, portfolio-margin engines, volatility-data providers, smart-account tooling, automated strategy products, and the latest 2026 market structure, I would change the product thesis slightly from the previous answer.

The biggest opportunity is probably not to launch another options exchange first.

It is to build the execution, abstraction, risk, and automation layer that sits above existing options liquidity, aggregates it, and turns options into understandable financial outcomes. Once that layer owns meaningful order flow, it can progressively internalize execution, create strategy-level markets, launch a volatility index, introduce perpetual-option primitives, and eventually become its own clearing/prime layer.

That sequencing avoids the single hardest problem in derivatives:

Launching an empty exchange and trying to manufacture both users and market makers simultaneously.

1. Whatould actually build
For clarity, I will call the hypothetical product Volatility OS.

This is not a proposed brand name. It describes the architecture.

The user should not initially see:

The user should initially see:

Behind this extremely simple interface would sit a sophisticated derivatives engine.

The system architecture would be:

Embedded SDK	Put all of this inside wallets, exchanges and treasuries
This is why I think “options exchange” is too narrow a description.

The product is really a volatility operating system.

2. The key strategic insight: don't compete with Derive on Derive's strongest feature
Derive has already done much of the very difficult infrastructure work.

It offers portfolio margin, cross-asset collateral and multiple subaccounts, with its risk engine calculating maximum portfolio loss under spot and volatility shocks and adding contingencies for basis, oracles and other risks. Derive says its system recognizes offsets between multi-leg structures instead of margining every leg independently. (insights.derive.xyz)

Paradex is building in essentially the same professional direction. Its portfolio-margin system currently stress-tests portfolios across 24 simultaneous spot/IV scenarios, although portfolio margin remains beta/whitelisted. (docs.paradex.trade)

And Paradigm has already built sophisticated institutional multi-leg liquidity discovery. Paradigm's RFQ model supports private auctions, multiple makers and block-trade settlement in which all legs clear atomically or the entire trade is rejected. (Paradigm Documentation)

Therefore I would not spend the first two years recreating:

matching engine + appchain + margin engine + market-maker API + new token incentives

just to arrive where these projects already are.

Instead:

Use existing venues as liquidity backends and build the product layer they have not yet fully solved.

3. Why I think this is the correct wedge
The latest Block Scholes study reached a strikingly similar diagnosis after interviewing Derive, Rysk, Panoptic and GammaSwap builders.

The historical bottlenecks repeatedly came down to:

UX, demand, collateral efficiency, education, two-sided liquidity and professional market-making infrastructure.

The report explicitly argues that infrastructure improvements alone do not create options demand; users need products solving actual problems that perps or prediction markets do not solve as naturally. (blockscholes.com)

That is the opening.

The new company doesn't initially need to invent another option.

It needs to answer:

What are you trying to accomplish with your money?

and construct the derivatives beneath that objective.

4. Feature #1 — Intent-based optionshis is probably the most important consumer feature.
There is already precedent in traditional markets.

Interactive Brokers' options tools can start from a user's price or volatility forecast and generate relevant option strategies. Robinhood's strategy builder organizes multi-leg structures around market outlook and shows max gain/loss and payoff information.

So “intent → options strategy” itself isn't the moat.

The opportunity is extending it into:

crypto + multiple venues + persistent automation + portfolio context + actual executable quotes.

5. The compiler should be deterministic, not an LLM trading agent
AI is useful at the language interface.

For example:

“I own 5 ETH. I think ETH probably stays flat for a month, but I don't want to lose the coins if it rallies massively.”

An LLM can translate that into a structured intent:

Field	Parsed value
Underlying	ETH
Existing holding	+5 ETH
Bias	Neutral/slightly bullish
Goal	Income
Constraint	Preserve exposure beyond large upside
Candidate structures	covered call, call spread overlay, etc.
But the LLM should not calculate the option price, margin requirement, Greeks or risk.

Those should come from deterministic financial libraries and venue data.

The chain should be:

natural language → structured schema → deterministic strategy engine → deterministic risk engine → firm market quotes → user authorization.

That's a much safer architecture than:

“AI thinks this trade looks good → sign transaction.”

8. Routing should occur at the strategy level
This is a big distinction from a conventional aggregator.

Suppose the user wants:

100 BTC 100k/120k call spreads.

A naïve router says:

Venue A has the cheapest 100k calls.
Venue B has the best bid for 120k calls.

That can be disastrous.

You buy the calls on A.

BTC jumps.

Before you sell the calls on B, the market moves.

You've acquired enormous unintended delta and vega exposure.

So the objective function should be:

best executable price for the entire payoff.

Paradigm already demonstrates the importance of this concept. Its block trades are sent to settlement venues atomically; if one leg cannot clear, the block is rejected. Paradex's own RFQ API now supports up to 25 legs, including strategy legs and fixed-price hedge legs. (Paradigm Documentation)

This tells us that strategy-native trading is technically quite realistic.

9. How the router should decide where to send an order
Execution could roughly follow:

Trade	Preferred execution
Small liquid single option	CLOB
Standard spread	Combo/strategy book
Large spread	Multi-dealer RFQ
Very large institutional position	Private RFQ
Long-tail token	AMM/perpetual primitive
Highly customized strategy	Solver / RFQ
Automated roll	Calendar-combo/RFQ
Delta-neutral structure	Options + fixed hedge leg
The cost function shouldn't simply be:

lowest premium.

It should approximately be:

**option price

venue fees
gas
bid/ask slippage
expected hedge slippage
collateral opportunity cost
borrowing/funding
settlement risk
bridge/transfer cost.**
That is much closer to true execution quality.

10. Solver competition could become the next evolution of options RFQ
The architecture of CoW Protocol and intent systems provides an interesting model.

CoW uses combinatorial batch auctions and competing solvers to source liquidity. More generally, ERC-7683 now describes a solver-facing framework in which a user specifies an outcome and specialized fillers determine how to fulfill it. (docs.cow.fi)

Imagine applying that model to an option strategy.

The user signs:

Deliver +100 BTC 100k calls
and −100 BTC 120k calls
expiring October 30
net debit ≤ $820,000
before 14:
before 14:01:30 UTC.

Several solvers compete.

Solver A may execute entirely on Derive.

Solver B may warehouse the option risk and hedge through Deribit.

Solver C may quote using Paradex and its own inventory.

Solver D may cross against another client intent.

The user doesn't care.

They receive the desired economic position at the best eligible firm price.

This is potentially much bigger than a simple DEX aggregator.

11. But cross-venue atomicity is the hardest problem
This needs to be stated explicitly because many “aggregator” ideas hide it.

A trade cannot magically be atomic across:

Derive + Paradex + Deribit + a CEX + an AMM

unless some intermediary takes settlement risk.

Therefore cross-venue strategy execution ultimately requires one of three models.

Model	How it works	Trade-off
Same-venue package	Entire strategy stays on one venue	Safest, less price competition
Sequential routing	Router executes legs in sequence	Leg risk
Principal solver	Solver gives user firm package price and hedges wherever it wants	Solver needs capital and risk infrastructure
The third model is the most powerful.

It is also where the moat develops.

The solver is effectively saying:

“I guarantee you the strategy at this price. Whatever happens while I hedge it is my problem.”

Now sophisticated market makers become part of the product's infrastructure.

12. Feature #3 — atomic strategy markets
This should be treated as a first-class primitive.

An iron condor isn't four unrelated options.

It's one economic position.

A collar isn't three positions.

It's one risk-management transaction.

Paradigm's current public orderbook concept already supports globally available strategies containing multiple instruments and quantity ratios, while its RFQ workflow supports private one- or multi-leg structures. (Paradigm Documentation)

Your internal object therefore shouldn't be:

Order

It should be:

StrategyOrder.

Conceptually:

Parameter	Example
Legs	Four
Ratios	+1 / −1 / −1 / +1
Net direction	Buy
Quantity	100
Limit	$X debit
FOK	True
Allowed venues	Derive, Paradex
Hedge leg	Optional BTC perp
Anonymous	Yes
Quote validity	5 sec
Maximum slippage	5 bps equivalent
Margin pre-check	Passed
Every quote returned by a market maker would be for the net package price.

That makes option strategies behave more like perpetual-futures orders.

13. This also creates “strategy liquidity”
Today liquidity is fragmented among thousands of instruments.

But users repeatedly trade:

BTC call spread.

ETH put spread.

BTC straddle.

BTC collar.

ETH covered call.

You can eventually create persistent markets in standardized structures.

For example:

BTC 30D 25Δ put spread

instead of forcing users to independently locate two strikes.

This allows liquidity to concentrate around an economic strategy rather than individual contracts.

That's a significant potential improvement to the existing architecture.

16. Different perpetual-option designs require different carry models
This is another reason the abstraction layer matters.

Panoptic's economics aren't identical to Paradex's documented perpetual-option model or GammaSwap's borrowing model.

Panoptic uses AMM-native perpetual exposures with streaming premia; Block Scholes describes Panoptic premia as path-dependent and connected to AMM behavior. (Block Scholes)

Therefore the abstraction layer should normalize everything into:

Expected carrying cost.

The user doesn't need to understand every implementation.

But the advanced screen can show:

protocol premium

borrow component

utilization

AMM fee component

volatility component

path dependency

and confidence range.

17. “Maximum carry” can become an order type
This could be genuinely useful.

Existing markets mostly let you control:

price;

quantity;

slippage.

For perpetual options, create:

Max Carry Order
For example:

Maintain this BTC convex position only while projected 7-day carry is below $200.

or:

Never allow cumulative funding to exceed 15% of original premium equivalent.

or:

Convert to the cheapest equivalent 30-day dated option when perpetual carry becomes more expensive.

Now perpetual and dated options can compete dynamically.

18. Feature #5 — persistent positions / Auto-Roll
I think this could actually become more important than “true” perpetual options.

Instead of inventing a new derivative, give a dated option a perpetual user experience.

The user asks:

“Always keep BTC protected 20% below spot.”

The system maintains the exposure indefinitely.

Underneath it might hold:

today:

30D 80%-moneyness BTC put

and when 7 DTE remains:

sell it and buy:

new 30D 80%-moneyness put.

Traditional finance already uses systematic rolling extensively; Cboe maintains rule-based option indices with explicit roll methodologies, and institutional broker tools already automate/assist option rolls.

The opportunity is to make this native to crypto portfolios.

19. There should be several roll policies
A persistent option position needs a policy definition.

Policy	Meaning
Constant maturity	Always maintain ~30 DTE
Constant delta	Always maintain ~25Δ put
Constant moneyness	Always maintain 20% OTM
Constant premium	Spend $500/month
Constant portfolio protection	Target max portfolio drawdown
Constant gamma	Maintain target convexity
Event-aware	Don't roll through specified event
Carry-aware	Choose dated/perpetual based on cost
This is where options become dramatically more useful for wealth/treasury management.

20. Rolling must itself be atomic
You don't want:

sell old put → market crashes → cannot buy new put.

So a roll should be represented as a calendar spread package:

sell old protection

buy new protection
at one net price.

Again, strategy execution solves the problem.

21. Auto-roll needs strict execution rules
Automation should be allowed to skip a roll if conditions are unacceptable.

Example policy:

Guardrail	Rule
Max spread	3% premium
Max roll debit	$2,000
Minimum maker quotes	3
Minimum quote size	100% required
Oracle confidence	High
Maximum IV percentile	95th
Emergency condition	Never go completely unhedged
If liquidity is bad, don't blindly execute because a cron job fired.

This is one reason an options automation system needs a risk engine, not merely a scheduler.

22. Feature #6 — a native composite crypto volatility index
This remains one of the strongest infrastructure opportunities, but the research changed my view somewhat.

I would not simply build another IV data provider.

Block Scholes already aggregates derivatives data from a large collection of venues and offers SVI-calibrated volatility surfaces, APIs and onchain signed oracle data. (Block Scholes)

The opportunity is therefore narrower and more valuable:

Create the canonical settlement and risk indices for the onchain volatility ecosystem.

That is different from selling analytics.

But strategically it means the “decentralized” options ecosystem still depends substantially on centralized volatility discovery.

26. Confidence must be first-class data
Imagine:

Derive's existing PM design already explicitly considers oracle confidence as part of margin contingencies, illustrating why confidence information matters economically, not just analytically. (Derive Insights)

27. The real moat is adoption of the index, not construction of it
An index can be copied.

A settlement standard cannot easily be copied once:

lenders use it;

options venues use it for margin;

structured products reference it;

risk dashboards report it;

volatility futures settle against it.

That creates network effects.

The ambition shouldn't be:

“we have great data.”

It should be:

“BTCVOL30 is the number everyone means when they talk about 30-day onchain BTC volatility.”

32. Feature #8 — unified collateral and volatility prime brokerage
This is potentially the largest institutional opportunity.

It is also the one most often described too casually.

You cannot simply claim:

“Portfolio margin across Derive, Paradex and Deribit.”

Each venue independently controls its collateral.

If one venue liquidates you, the fact that another account has excess collateral doesn't necessarily save you.

Therefore there are four different products hiding behind the phrase “unified margin.”

Level	Product	Difficulty
1	Unified portfolio view	Low
2	Automated collateral allocator	Medium
3	Prime vault / credit intermediary	Very high
4	Common clearinghouse	Extreme
This distinction matters enormously.

33. Level 1 — unified portfolio view
This is immediately feasible.

User connects:

Derive;

Paradex;

CEX accounts if permitted;

wallet.

The system shows:

net delta

net gamma

net vega

margin by venue

liquidation buffer

collateral utilization

concentration

funding

stress P&L.

This alone is valuable.

Institutional crypto infrastructure companies such as Talos already demonstrate demand for cross-venue portfolio/risk and execution infrastructure. The opportunity is to make that especially strong for options/onchain volatility.

34. Level 2 — collateral allocator
Now the software can actually move assets.

Example:

Derive margin utilization: 83%.

Paradex: 14%.

Wallet: $600k USDC idle.

Policy says:

Keep every venue ≥35% above projected maintenance requirement.

The system moves $200k to Derive.

For onchain venues this can potentially be automated with smart accounts.

But there are problems:

bridge delay;

chain congestion;

withdrawal delay;

oracle changes during transfer;

reorgs;

venue maintenance.

So the system needs an emergency collateral reserve, not 100% efficiency.

35. Level 3 — real prime brokerage
This is much more difficult.

The user deposits collateral with a prime layer.

The prime layer allocates credit/margin to several venues.

Now the user sees:

$10M collateral → one account.

The prime decides:

$4M Derive capacity.

$2M Paradex capacity.

$3M institutional/CEX capacity.

$1M liquidity reserve.

This creates enormous efficiency.

But the prime layer now assumes:

counterparty risk;

liquidity risk;

credit risk;

custody risk;

legal obligations.

This isn't merely a smart-contract feature.

It becomes a financial institution.

36. Cross-venue portfolio margin is potentially the ultimate moat
Suppose the user's true portfolio is:

+100 BTC spot
−100 BTC perpetual
+100 80k puts
−100 70k puts.

On separate venues, each piece consumes capital.

A common risk engine knows that much of this exposure cancels.

That could produce enormous capital savings.

But you need the ability to enforce claims across every venue before you can safely give the savings.

Otherwise you've simply created unsecured leverage.

So I would not build this first.

I would earn the right to build it after owning substantial execution flow and risk data.

37. Feature #9 — long-tail options
This remains a major gap.

Traditional option CLOBs are extremely inefficient for a small token because every token creates:

multiple expiries × many strikes × calls/puts.

Paradex demonstrates just how many dimensions a normal options listing creates: it lists daily, weekly and monthly expiries and dynamically lists strikes based on delta bands. (Paradex Documentation)

For BTC this is reasonable.

For token #187 by market cap it is probably absurd.

That's why the long-tail product needs a different architecture.

38. I would support three long-tail modes
AMM-native perpetual options
Panoptic-like architecture.

Best where the token already has deep AMM liquidity.

Panoptic explicitly uses AMM liquidity to create perpetual option exposure, which can extend optionality into assets that do not support conventional options markets. Block Scholes' research found this can potentially reach assets such as SHIB and PEPE that are not well represented on centralized options venues. (Block Scholes)

Request-to-create RFQ
No standing options chain.

User asks:

Quote me $100k of 30-day HYPE 20% OTM puts.

Three makers respond.

If none respond, no market exists.

This avoids fragmenting liquidity into thousands of empty orderbooks.

Binary/touch products
For extremely short-dated retail speculation:

SOL above $300 at 16:00?

or:

BTC touches $120k in the next 24 hours?

These sacrifice option flexibility for extreme simplicity.

Block Scholes' 2026 market review specifically notes that newer binary/touch products are simplifying convex trades into outcomes that compete more directly with perps and prediction markets. (Block Scholes)

39. Long-tail assets should NOT receive the same margin treatment as BTC
This is where a lot of protocols eventually get hurt.

For a new asset:

isolated margin

low position cap

high collateral haircut

full collateralization where necessary

strict oracle confidence

limited short-option exposure

lower max utilization

no cross-collateral benefit

until liquidity proves itself.

Then risk parameters can relax gradually.

Permissionless listing does not have to mean permissionless contagion.

This could become the major distribution engine.

Most users shouldn't have to discover an “options exchange.”

The option should appear exactly where the problem exists.

For example:

Lending app

The user doesn't visit Volatility OS.

Volatility OS visits the user.

42. Embedded distribution creates a much stronger moat than a frontend
Anybody can clone a frontend.

It is much harder to replace a network where:

44. Privacy should be built into the RFQ architecture
A large institution does not want to advertise:

BUY 5,000 BTC PUTS.

The RFQ should support:

anonymous identity;

hidden size where appropriate;

limited counterparty set;

short quote lifetime;

no public pre-trade signal.

Paradigm's RFQ workflow already allows disclosed or anonymous taker RFQs and keeps competing maker quotes private from other makers. (Paradigm Documentation)

A serious onchain product needs equivalent functionality.

“Everything public because blockchain” is not a viable institutional market-structure strategy.

Settlement transparency and pre-trade privacy can coexist.

45. Feature #12 — agentic options management
This is one of the more exciting areas, but it needs to be built differently from most “AI trading agent” products.

Do NOT give an AI:

wallet private key + unrestricted execution.

Instead create:

AI proposes. Deterministic policy engine approves. Smart contract enforces.

That distinction is fundamental.

46. Smart accounts make this architecture feasible now
Safe modules can execute automated/custom logic and support restrictions including allowlists, rate limits and spending caps. Safe guards can inspect transactions before execution and block them when policy conditions fail. ERC-4337 similarly supports smart accounts with custom validation logic around UserOperations. (docs.safe.global)

This means an agent could have authorization to:

roll BTC puts

but not:

withdraw BTC.

Or:

hedge delta with BTC perps

but neverceed $100,000 notional.

That is much safer.

47. The policy engine should sit above the agent
Example:

Policy	Constraint
Allowed assets	BTC, ETH
Allowed protocols	Derive, Paradex
Max gross notional	$2M
Max premium/day	$10k
Max weekly carry	$20k
Minimum margin buffer	40%
Max net delta	±2 BTC
Max short gamma	X
Max short vega	Y
Naked short options	Forbidden
Max slippage	40 bps
Withdrawal	Forbidden
New contract approvals	Human signature
Policy modifications	2-of-3 multisig
Emergency stop	Owner anytime
Then the agent can operate freely inside the box.

That's the right agent architecture.

48. Four very useful agents could emerge
This is the one list I'll use because the distinction matters:

Hedge Agent — monitors portfolio delta/protection and trades approved instruments to maintain configured risk.
Roll Agent — maintains persistent options such as 30D crash protection or continuously covered calls.
Carry Agent — chooses between equivalent perpetual and dated structures based on actual carry/execution cost.
Volatility Agent — maintains a predefined volatility exposure such as delta-neutral long gamma while respecting vega/gamma/capital constraints.
The user defines the policy.

The agent manages implementation.

49. The agent should never be the final risk authority
Example:

Agent decides:

Buy 100 ETH puts.

Before execution:

Then:

policy engine checks limits.

Then:

smart-account guard verifies allowable destination/function/value.

Then:

execution router gets quotes.

Then:

trade executes.

This creates multiple independent safety boundaries.

Safe itself warns that modules and guards are extremely security-critical and that badly designed guards/modules can cause loss or denial of service, so recovery, auditing and restricted permissions are mandatory. (Safe Docs)

52. Risk engine architecture
This is the part I would invest heavily in.

You cannot build the product above without a serious risk engine.

Paradex's current SCAN architecture gives a useful reference: portfolio risk is computed across 24 simultaneous spot and volatility shocks. (Paradex Documentation)

The new engine should extend that concept across:

skew

term structure

forward basis

perp funding

collateral price

collateral depeg

correlation

liquidity

oracle confidence.

A portfolio could therefore be tested against scenarios such as:

Scenario	BTC	ETH	IV	Stablecoin	Liquidity

Melt-up	+35%	+50%	+10%	1.00	medium

Basis shock	−10%	−12%	+20%	1.00	medium

Then margin corresponds to plausible worst loss plus buffers.

53. Liquidation should preserve strategy topology
This is very important.

Suppose:

+100 $100k calls

−100 $120k calls.

It is a defined-risk spread.

An unintelligent liquidation engine might sell the long calls first.

Suddenly:

−100 naked $120k calls.

Risk explodes.

Liquidation therefore needs to operate on risk groups:

close 100 spreads.

not:

close whichever position appears first.

Paradex's options liquidation documentation similarly emphasizes reducing exposures while preserving hedge relationships where possible; this is the direction a sophisticated risk engine should take.

54. Build a “risk compiler” alongside the strategy compiler
The strategy compiler asks:

What positions achieve the user outcome?

The risk compiler asks:

What constraints make those positions safe to execute and automate?

Every proposed strategy receives:

margin impact;

liquidation impact;

collateral concentration;

max loss;

stress loss;

vega;

gamma;

carry;

oracle dependency;

exit-liquidity score.

If the strategy cannot pass the risk compiler, it never reaches execution.

55. Exit liquidity needs to become a displayed metric
Options platforms currently emphasize entry price.

But options are notorious for becoming illiquid after spot moves away from a strike.

So each instrument/strategy should have a:

Potential components:

current spread;

depth;

historical RFQ response;

open interest;

strike distance;

time to expiry;

expected unwind slippage.

The user might see:

That's much more informative than an attractive opening premium alone.

72. Regulation has to influence the architecture from day one
For a product serving U.S. retail users, derivatives access is not simply a frontend question. The CFTC states that a trading facility offering futures/options on commodities to non-eligible contract participants generally needs to operate as a designated contract market unless an exclusion or exemption applies. (cftc.gov)

The regulatory structure is also changing quickly. On May 29, 2026, CFTC staff issued specific 24/7 trading/clearing guidance and separately enabled a path under specified conditions for Coinbase Financial Markets to intermediate certain foreign crypto derivatives through Deribit. TC](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9239-26?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So jurisdiction and user eligibility should be an architectural service:

user → jurisdiction/eligibility → allowable venue → allowable instruments → allowable execution route.

Not:

build globally and add compliance later.

For a startup, one reasonable architecture is to begin primarily as non-custodial technology/execution infrastructure integrating legally available venues for eligible users, rather than immediately attempting to become the legal counterparty, clearinghouse and retail derivatives exchange everywhere. The precise structure would require specialist counsel in each target jurisdiction.

78. The deepest market gap
After looking at all of these projects together, I think the gap can be stated very precisely:

Crypto has increasingly good venues for trading options, but it still lacks a universal layer that converts user outcomes into executable, automated, cross-venue volatility positions.

Derive is solving exchange/risk infrastructure.

Paradigm is solving institutional liquidity discovery.

Paradex is solving unified derivatives infrastructure and sophisticated RFQ/margin.

“A decentralized perpetual options exchange.”

It should be:

“Tell us the risk or payoff you want. We construct it, find the liquidity, execute it atomically, manage the collateral, and keep the exposure within the limits you set.”

And underneath:

Derive where Derive is best.

Paradex where Paradex is best.

Paradigm/RFQ where blocks are best.

AMM/perpetual systems where long-tail exposure is best.

Eventually:

your own liquidity where you have enough order flow to justify it.

That is a much more defensible company.

80. The highest-value proprietary innovation
If I had to reduce all of the research to one product innovation, it would be this:

Persistent Strategy Intents
Instead of placing an order, a user creates a policy.

Example:

BTC Protection Policy

Protect 80% of my BTC holdings against losses beyond 20%.
Keep protection between 21 and 45 days maturity.
Spend no more than $1,000/month.
Use only defined-risk positions.
Route between approved venues.
Never trade when oracle confidence is below 95%.
Require at least two market-maker quotes.
Maximum execution slippage 1.5%.
Maintain this until I cancel.

Now the product isn't competing with Derive on its option chain.

Derive becomes one of the engines powering the policy.

The user's relationship is with your risk policy, not with a specific contract.

That is much harder for a traditional exchange to displace.

81. And this naturally solves perpetual options
This is the most interesting consequence.

The user doesn't have to choose:

dated option vs perpetual option vs Panoptic vs auto-roll.

They choose:

persistent convexity.

Your engine can evaluate:

dated option + roll cost;

perpetual option + expected carry;

AMM-native option;

structured spread;

and continuously choose among the implementations permitted by the user's policy.

At that point:

“perpetual option” stops being a contract type and becomes a user experience.

I think that is a much more powerful direction.

Final conclusion
The research suggests that the next major crypto-options company should probably not begin as another exchange. Exchange infrastructure is finally becoming fairly sophisticated, while user abstraction, cross-venue execution, persistent risk policies and distribution remain badly fragmented.

The strongest product architecture is therefore:

Intent UX
→ Strategy Compiler
→ Volatility/Risk Engine
→ Strategy-Level RFQ & Solver Router
→ Existing Liquidity Venues
→ Smart-Account Policy Engine
→ Auto-Roll / Carry Management
→ Embedded APIs
→ Market-Maker OS
→ Composite Volatility Indices
→ Collateral Optimization
→ eventually Prime Clearing + Native Perpetual/Long-Tail Markets.

The especially valuable innovations are strategy-level rather than contract-level: atomic package execution, carry budgets, persistent hedges, auto-roll, outcome APIs, constrained autonomous agents and eventually a standardized Strategy Quote Protocol.
